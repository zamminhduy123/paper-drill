    Proposed Research Splits

    Split 1: Attack Stealthiness and Detection Difficulty

    Datasets: ROAD (for stealthy attacks) + Car-Hacking (for baseline attacks)

    Rationale: ROAD's flam fabrication attacks inject only a single malicious frame after each legitimate message with the target ID, making them the most stealthy public attacks available - 17 . Car-Hacking's DoS (0.3 ms intervals) and Fuzzy attacks (random IDs/payloads every 0.5 ms) are comparatively detectable - 1 . This split isolates detection sensitivity from false positive behavior —a limitation explicitly unaddressed in the provided abstract.

    Experimental Design:

      * Train deep learning models (LSTM, Autoencoder, CNN) on ambient/normal data from both datasets separately.

      * Evaluate on progressively stealthy attack variants: (a) Car-Hacking Fuzzy/DoS, (b) Car-Hacking Spoofing, (c) ROAD Fuzzing, (d) ROAD Fabrication, (e) ROAD Simulated Masquerade.

      * Measure detection rate as a function of injection frequency (frames per second of attack traffic).

    Expected Contribution: Quantify the detection floor for deep learning models when attack traffic is nearly indistinguishable from ambient traffic in volume.

    Split 2: Cross-Vehicle Generalization

    Datasets: Car-Hacking (Hyundai) + CIC-IoV-2024 (Ford)

    Rationale: Both datasets capture CAN traffic from real vehicles, but from different manufacturers with different ECU configurations and ID mappings. Car-Hacking anonymizes nothing about the vehicle source - 1 , while CIC-IoV-2024 explicitly describes a 2019 Ford with intact ECU structure - 13 . This split tests whether deep learning models learn generalizable CAN patterns or memorize vehicle-specific ID sequences .

    Experimental Design:

      * Train on Car-Hacking (combined normal + attack).

      * Test zero-shot on CIC-IoV-2024 without fine-tuning.

      * Then reverse: train on CIC-IoV-2024, test on Car-Hacking.

      * Fine-tune with small fraction (1%, 5%, 10%) of target-domain normal data.

      * Compare feature representations: raw CAN IDs/payloads vs. normalized features (timing, entropy, frequency).

    Expected Contribution: Establish whether cross-vehicle transfer is feasible, and how much target-domain data is needed for adaptation.

    Split 3: Attack Type Generalization

    Datasets: CAN-ML + Car-Hacking

    Rationale: CAN-ML contains seven attack types (DoS, fuzzing, interval, speed, standstill, systematic) - 4 , while Car-Hacking has four (DoS, Fuzzy, RPM spoof, Gear spoof) - 1 . This split tests open-set recognition : can a model trained on some attack types detect novel attack types it has never seen?

    Experimental Design:

      * Leave-one-attack-type-out cross-validation within each dataset.

      * Train on all attack types except one; test detection on the held-out type.

      * Compare supervised classifiers vs. one-class anomaly detectors (trained only on normal).

      * Measure: (a) detection of known attacks, (b) detection of unseen attacks, (c) false positive rate on normal data.

    Expected Contribution: Determine whether deep learning models learn attack-invariant features or overfit to signatures of specific attack types.

    Split 4: Class Imbalance and Attack Scarcity

    Datasets: Car-Hacking (14.2M normal : 2.3M attack) + CIC-IoV-2024 (86.9% benign) - 1 - 8

    Rationale: Both datasets exhibit severe class imbalance, and the provided abstract does not describe how this is addressed. Real in-vehicle attacks are rare; models must perform well with few positive examples. This split explicitly tests the low-data regime for attack detection.

    Experimental Design:

      * Under-sample training attacks to 10%, 5%, 1%, 0.1% of original counts.

      * Compare: (a) standard cross-entropy, (b) focal loss, (c) class-weighted loss, (d) one-class training on normal only, (e) data augmentation via VAE/GAN.

      * Evaluate on fixed test set with original class distribution.

      * Report precision-recall AUC (not accuracy) due to imbalance.

    Expected Contribution: Establish how few labeled attack examples are needed, and which training strategies are robust to extreme imbalance.

    Split 5: Temporal Generalization Across Driving Conditions

    Datasets: ROAD (ambient captures across dynamometer + road) + Car-Hacking

    Rationale: ROAD includes ambient data collected on both a dynamometer and real roads, with varied driving activities (including unusual events like opened door while driving) - 17 . Car-Hacking captures a fixed attack scenario - 1 . This split tests whether models trained on one driving condition generalize to another—addressing the latency and deployment constraints not described in the provided abstract.

    Experimental Design:

      * Train on ROAD dynamometer ambient + attacks.

      * Test on ROAD road ambient (false positive behavior under natural variation).

      * Reverse: train on road, test on dynamometer.

      * For Car-Hacking: train on normal, test on attack segments split by time (early vs. late).

      * Measure false positive rate specifically during "unusual but normal" events (e.g., opened door, rapid acceleration).

    Expected Contribution: Quantify false positive inflation under domain shift, and whether temporal context reduces it.
